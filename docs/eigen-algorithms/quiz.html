Quiz: how eigen() works, and big problems

Short questions to test your understanding of the QR algorithm, eigenvalue sensitivity and Krylov methods. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. One step of the QR algorithm replaces \(A_k = Q_kR_k\) by \(A_{k+1} = R_kQ_k\). Why does this not change the eigenvalues?

Hint

Write \(R_k\) in terms of \(Q_k\) and \(A_k\).

Answer

\(R_k = Q_k^TA_k\), so \(A_{k+1} = Q_k^TA_kQ_k\): a similarity transform with an orthogonal matrix. Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues (\(A_{k+1}\,(Q_k^T\mathbf v) = \lambda\,Q_k^T\mathbf v\) whenever \(A_k\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\)). Because \(Q_k\) is orthogonal the transform also does not magnify rounding errors.

Q2. A symmetric matrix has eigenvalues 5, 4 and 1. Running the unshifted QR algorithm, which subdiagonal entry goes to zero faster, \(a_{21}\) or \(a_{32}\)? About how many steps does each need to fall from 1 to \(10^{-8}\)?

Hint

\(a_{i+1,i}\) shrinks like \(|\lambda_{i+1}/\lambda_i|^k\). Solve \(r^k = 10^{-8}\) with logarithms.

Answer

\(a_{21}\): ratio \(4/5 = 0.8\), \(k = 8/\log_{10}(1.25) \approx 83\) steps. \(a_{32}\): ratio \(1/4\), \(k = 8/\log_{10}4 \approx 13\) steps. So \(a_{32}\) is much faster. The close pair 5 and 4 is the bottleneck, which is why real implementations use shifts.

Q3. Why does LAPACK first reduce a matrix to Hessenberg (or, if symmetric, tridiagonal) form before starting the QR iteration?

Hint

Compare the cost of one QR step on a full matrix and on a Hessenberg matrix, and ask whether the form survives a step.

Answer

A QR step on a full \(n \times n\) matrix costs \(O(n^3)\); on a Hessenberg matrix only \(O(n^2)\) (one Givens rotation per subdiagonal entry), and on a tridiagonal one \(O(n)\). The reduction costs \(O(n^3)\) but is done only once, and QR steps keep the Hessenberg (tridiagonal) form, so all later steps are cheap.

Q4. What does a shift \(\mu\) do in the QR algorithm, and why is \(\mu = a_{nn}\) a good choice? Give a matrix where it fails.

Hint

The rate for the last subdiagonal entry is \(|\lambda_n - \mu|/|\lambda_{n-1} - \mu|\). Try \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\).

Answer

The step is done on \(A - \mu I\), whose eigenvalues are \(\lambda_i - \mu\); then \(\mu I\) is added back. If \(\mu\) is close to an eigenvalue \(\lambda_n\), the ratio \(|\lambda_n - \mu|/|\lambda_{n-1} - \mu|\) is tiny and the last row converges in a few steps. \(a_{nn}\) is a Rayleigh quotient, so it approaches \(\lambda_n\) as the iteration proceeds: convergence becomes quadratic (cubic for symmetric matrices). It fails for \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\): \(a_{22} = 2\) lies exactly between the eigenvalues 1 and 3, so \(A - 2I\) has eigenvalues \(\pm1\) of equal modulus and nothing happens. The Wilkinson shift (the eigenvalue of the trailing 2 × 2 block nearer to \(a_{nn}\)) avoids this.

Q5. eigen() of a real 3 × 3 Jacobian returns -0.2+1.1i, -0.2-1.1i, -0.5+0i. A colleague suspects a rounding problem and wants to keep only the real parts. What do you say?

Hint

Can a real matrix have complex eigenvalues? What do they mean for \(\dot{\mathbf x} = J\mathbf x\)?

Answer

The complex pair is the correct answer, not noise: real matrices can have complex eigenvalues, always in conjugate pairs. The steady state is a stable spiral: perturbations oscillate with period \(2\pi/1.1 \approx 5.7\) time units and decay like \(e^{-0.2t}\). Dropping the imaginary parts would lose the oscillation. (Rounding noise looks different: imaginary parts of size \(10^{-15}\) on a matrix that should be symmetric. Then say symmetric = TRUE.)

Q6. A cell-maturation model has 20 transit compartments in a chain, all with rate 1 per day. In exact arithmetic all eigenvalues are \(-1\). Roughly how far from \(-1\) may the eigenvalues that eigen() returns lie, given rounding errors of about \(10^{-16}\) in the entries? What would the answer be for a symmetric matrix?

Hint

For a chain of \(n\) compartments with a perturbation \(\varepsilon\), the eigenvalues move by about \(\varepsilon^{1/n}\).

Answer

\((10^{-16})^{1/20} = 10^{-0.8} \approx 0.16\): the computed eigenvalues may sit anywhere on a circle of radius about 0.16 around \(-1\), some with imaginary parts. For a symmetric matrix the change is at most the size of the perturbation, about \(10^{-16}\).

Q7. Name two checks you can run to see whether the eigenvalues of a nonsymmetric matrix are trustworthy.

Hint

One uses the eigenvectors; the other uses only eigen() and random numbers.

Answer
  1. The condition number of the eigenvector matrix, kappa(eigen(A)$vectors) (Python np.linalg.cond(np.linalg.eig(A)[1])): eigenvalues can move by up to \(\kappa(V)\) times the perturbation (Bauer–Fike); near 1 is safe, \(10^9\) means up to 9 digits lost. (2) Perturb and recompute: add random changes at the level of the uncertainty of the entries and see how far the eigenvalues move. For the 10-compartment chain, \(10^{-12}\) in gives \(5\times10^{-5}\) out.

Q8. What do these lines print, and why? (\(P\) is the transition matrix of a cell that switches state every generation.)

// rust/examples/eigen_algorithms_q8.rs  —  cargo run --example eigen_algorithms_q8
// Quiz Q8: the QR algorithm on the period-2 transition matrix, unshifted and shifted.
use nalgebra::Matrix2;

fn show(m: &Matrix2<f64>) {
    for i in 0..2 { println!("{:10.6} {:10.6}", m[(i, 0)], m[(i, 1)]); }
}

fn main() {
    let p: Matrix2<f64> = Matrix2::new(0.0, 1.0,
                                       1.0, 0.0);
    let qr = p.qr();
    show(&(qr.r() * qr.q()));                         // one unshifted step
    let mu = 0.5;
    let eye = Matrix2::<f64>::identity();
    let mut p1 = p;
    for _ in 0..10 {                                  // shifted steps, shift 0.5
        let qr = (p1 - eye * mu).qr();
        p1 = qr.r() * qr.q() + eye * mu;
    }
    show(&p1);
}

// Output:
//   0.000000   1.000000
//   1.000000   0.000000
//  -1.000000   0.000034
//   0.000034   1.000000
Hint

The eigenvalues of \(P\) are \(\pm1\). What are the eigenvalues of \(P - 0.5I\), and their ratio?

Answer

The unshifted step returns \(P\) itself: the columns of \(P\) are orthonormal, so \(Q = \pm P\), \(R = \pm I\), \(RQ = P\), and the eigenvalues \(\pm1\) have equal modulus, so the QR algorithm never makes progress. With shift 0.5 the shifted eigenvalues are \(-1.5\) and \(0.5\) (ratio 1/3), so the off-diagonal shrinks by about 1/3 per step: after 10 steps it is \(3.4\times10^{-5}\) and the diagonal shows \(-1\) and \(1\). (The outputs are printed in the code tabs.)

Q9. Lanczos and other Krylov methods need only one thing from the matrix. What is it, and why does that matter for PCA of a sparse cells × genes matrix?

Hint

Look at the line of the Lanczos loop that uses \(A\).

Answer

Products \(A\mathbf v\). For PCA, \(A = X_c^TX_c\) and \(A\mathbf v = X_c^T(X_c\mathbf v)\), with \(X_c\mathbf v = X\mathbf v - \mathbf 1(\boldsymbol\mu^T\mathbf v)\) and \(X_c^T\mathbf w = X^T\mathbf w - \boldsymbol\mu(\mathbf 1^T\mathbf w)\). So the sparse \(X\) is never centred (which would make it dense) and the covariance matrix is never formed. Memory stays at the size of \(X\) plus a few dozen vectors, and each step costs about \(4\,\text{nnz}(X)\) operations.

Q10. A hand-written Lanczos run on a covariance matrix reports the top Ritz values 41.2, 41.2, 41.2, 17.5, 17.5, 9.3. What has probably happened, and how do you fix it?

Hint

What happens to the orthogonality of the Lanczos vectors in floating point once a Ritz value has converged?

Answer

Loss of orthogonality: once a Ritz value converges, the Lanczos vectors drift back towards its eigenvector and the eigenvalue reappears as ghost copies. The matrix most likely has single eigenvalues 41.2 and 17.5. Fix: reorthogonalise each new vector against all previous ones (or use a library with restarting and selective reorthogonalisation: RSpectra, irlba, eigsh). Check: the residual \(\|A\mathbf y - \theta\mathbf y\|\), and whether the problem has any reason for a repeated eigenvalue.

Q11. irlba(X, nv = 10, center = mu) needed 84 products with \(X\); asking for nv = 20 needed 336. The singular values are 335, 294, …, 218 for the first ten and 72.44, 72.41, 72.05, … after that. Explain.

Hint

Which eigenvalues does a Krylov method find quickly?

Answer

Krylov methods converge fast for extreme, well-separated eigenvalues. The first ten are well separated from the rest (a gap from 218 to 72). Numbers 11–20 lie inside a tight cluster of noise singular values, so separating them needs many more steps. They are also poorly determined (small changes in the data reorder them) and carry no cell-type signal, so the extra cost buys nothing.

Q12. For PCA of 100 000 cells × 2 000 genes (10 % non-zero), compare the memory needed by (a) the dense centred matrix, (b) the sparse matrix, (c) the gene–gene covariance matrix, (d) a cell–cell matrix. Which route would you take for the top 30 PCs?

Hint

8 bytes per double; about 12 bytes per non-zero in sparse storage.

Answer
  1. \(10^5 \cdot 2000 \cdot 8 = 1.6\) GB. (b) \(2\times10^7\) non-zeros × 12 bytes = 240 MB. (c) \(2000^2 \cdot 8 = 32\) MB, but forming it via a dense centred \(X\) costs (a). (d) \(10^{10} \cdot 8 = 80\) GB. Use a truncated, matrix-free method on the sparse matrix with implicit centring: irlba(X, nv = 30, center = colMeans(X)) or RSpectra::svds in R, scipy.sparse.linalg.svds with a LinearOperator in Python. Then check the residuals.
