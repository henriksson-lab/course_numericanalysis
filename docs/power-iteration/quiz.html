Quiz: power iteration and friends

Short questions to test your understanding of power iteration, the Rayleigh quotient and inverse iteration. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. Power iteration repeats \(\mathbf x \leftarrow A\mathbf x/\|A\mathbf x\|\). What does \(\mathbf x\) converge to, and what two conditions are needed?

Hint

Write \(\mathbf x_0 = c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 + \dots\) and multiply by \(A\) \(k\) times.

Answer

To the eigenvector \(\mathbf v_1\) of the eigenvalue of largest modulus. Needed: (1) that eigenvalue is strictly dominant, \(|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2|\); (2) the start has a component along it, \(c_1 \ne 0\). For a nonnegative Leslie or Markov matrix with a nonnegative start, (2) holds automatically, and (1) holds unless the life cycle or chain is periodic.

Q2. For a three-age-class Leslie matrix, eigen() returns \(\lambda_1 = 0.95\) and the first eigenvector \((-0.8, -0.5, -0.3)\). What do these tell a conservation biologist?

Hint

Eigenvectors come with arbitrary length and sign.

Answer

In the long run the population declines by 5 % per year. The stable age distribution is the eigenvector rescaled to sum 1: \((0.8, 0.5, 0.3)/1.6 = (0.50, 0.31, 0.19)\), half of the animals in the youngest class. The minus signs mean nothing: any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is an eigenvector.

Q3. A matrix has eigenvalues \(2\), \(-1.8\) and \(0.5\). At what rate does power iteration converge, and roughly how many iterations does each decimal digit take? What would you see in the eigenvalue estimates?

Hint

Rate \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|\); one digit takes \(1/\log_{10}|\lambda_1/\lambda_2|\) steps.

Answer

Rate \(1.8/2 = 0.9\); one digit takes \(1/\log_{10}(1/0.9) \approx 22\) iterations, so about 220 for 10 digits. Because \(\lambda_2\) is negative, the error alternates in sign: the estimates jump above and below \(\lambda_1 = 2\) while closing in on it.

Q4. Why does power iteration normalise \(\mathbf x\) every step? Does the normalisation change what it converges to?

Hint

How large is \(A^k\mathbf x\) after 5000 steps if \(\lambda_1 = 1.2\)? If \(\lambda_1 = 0.5\)?

Answer

\(A^k\mathbf x\) grows or shrinks like \(\lambda_1^k\), so without normalisation it overflows to Inf (then NaN) or underflows to 0. Normalising only rescales: the direction, which is what converges, is the same. Different norms (sum, 2-norm, max) give the same eigenvector with different scaling, and the eigenvalue estimate must be computed to match.

Q5. A biennial plant has \(L = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 4 \\ 0.25 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\). What do these lines print, and what is wrong?

  • R
  • Python
  • Rust
L <- matrix(c(0, 4, 0.25, 0), 2, byrow = TRUE)
for (steps in c(100, 101)) {
  x <- c(1, 0)
  for (k in 1:steps) { y <- drop(L %*% x); x <- y / sum(y) }
  cat(steps, x, "\n")
}
import numpy as np

L = np.array([[0, 4], [0.25, 0]])
for steps in [100, 101]:
    x = np.array([1.0, 0])
    for k in range(steps):
        y = L @ x
        x = y / y.sum()
    print(steps, x)
// rust/examples/power_iteration_q5.rs  —  cargo run --example power_iteration_q5
// Q5: power iteration on the biennial plant after 100 and 101 steps.
use nalgebra::{DMatrix, DVector};

fn main() {
    let l = DMatrix::from_row_slice(2, 2, &[0.0, 4.0, 0.25, 0.0]);
    for steps in [100, 101] {
        let mut x = DVector::from_row_slice(&[1.0, 0.0]);
        for _ in 0..steps {
            let y = &l * &x;
            x = &y / y.sum();
        }
        println!("{} {:?}", steps, x.as_slice());
    }
}
Hint

Compute \(L^2\), and the eigenvalues of \(L\).

Answer

After 100 steps \(\mathbf x = (1, 0)\), after 101 steps \((0, 1)\). \(L^2 = I\) and the eigenvalues are \(+1\) and \(-1\): equal modulus, so nothing dominates and the iteration cycles with period 2 forever (rosette year, flowering year). A fixed number of iterations without a convergence check would return either answer. Check the residual \(\|L\mathbf x - \lambda\mathbf x\|\), or look at Mod(eigen(L)$values).

Q6. Why is the largest eigenvalue of a Markov transition matrix always exactly 1? What then sets how fast a simulation of the chain reaches its stationary distribution?

Hint

What is \(P\) times a vector of ones?

Answer

Each row of \(P\) sums to 1, so \(P\mathbf 1 = \mathbf 1\): 1 is an eigenvalue of \(P\), and therefore of \(P^T\) (same eigenvalues). No eigenvalue can be larger in modulus, because multiplying by \(P\) cannot increase total probability. The rate is \(|\lambda_2|\): the deviation from \(\boldsymbol\pi\) shrinks by about \(|\lambda_2|\) per step. Biologically, \(|\lambda_2|\) sets the relaxation time of the population after a perturbation such as cell sorting.

Q7. A Markov chain simulation changes by at most \(10^{-9}\) in its last step, and the changes have been shrinking by a factor 0.999 per step. Roughly how far is it from the stationary distribution?

Hint

The remaining error is the sum of all future changes: a geometric series.

Answer

About \(10^{-9}\cdot 0.999/(1 - 0.999) \approx 10^{-6}\), a thousand times the last change. A small change per step is not a small error when the rate is close to 1. Use a residual or a rate-corrected estimate to stop, or speed things up with inverse iteration and a shift just above 1.

Q8. For a symmetric matrix, the current vector \(\mathbf x\) differs from the eigenvector by about \(10^{-4}\). Roughly how accurate is the Rayleigh quotient \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x/\mathbf x^T\mathbf x\) as an estimate of \(\lambda_1\)? And for a nonsymmetric matrix?

Hint

For symmetric matrices the eigenvectors are orthogonal, and the first-order error term cancels.

Answer

Symmetric: error of order \((10^{-4})^2 = 10^{-8}\) (times \(|\lambda_1 - \lambda_2|\)), so the eigenvalue has about twice as many correct digits as the vector. Nonsymmetric (e.g. a Leslie matrix): only of order \(10^{-4}\), no gain.

Q9. A matrix has eigenvalues \(5\), \(2\), \(1.9\) and \(0.1\). Which eigenvalue does inverse iteration find with shift \(\sigma = 2.1\), and at what rate? Which shift would you use to find \(0.1\) quickly?

Hint

The eigenvalues of \((A - \sigma I)^{-1}\) are \(1/(\lambda_i - \sigma)\); the rate is (distance to the nearest) / (distance to the next nearest).

Answer

\(\sigma = 2.1\) is nearest to \(2\) (distance 0.1); next nearest is \(1.9\) (distance 0.2). It finds \(\lambda = 2\) at rate \(0.1/0.2 = 0.5\): slow, because 2 and 1.9 are close. A shift closer to 2, such as 2.01, gives \(0.01/0.11 = 0.09\). For \(0.1\): \(\sigma = 0\) (plain inverse iteration) gives rate \(0.1/1.9 = 0.05\); \(\sigma = 0.1\) minus a little does even better.

Q10. In inverse iteration the shift is exactly an eigenvalue, so \(A - \sigma I\) is singular, or nearly so after rounding. Is the computed eigenvector then useless?

Hint

In which direction is the huge, inaccurate solution \(\mathbf y\) of \((A - \sigma I)\mathbf y = \mathbf x\) pointing?

Answer

No. The solution is huge and its size is meaningless, but it points almost exactly along the eigenvector, because \((A - \sigma I)^{-1}\) magnifies that direction by far more than any other. After normalising, one step gives nearly full precision (the cell-state chain with \(\sigma = 1 + 10^{-15}\): 14 digits). The only practical problem is that some solvers (R’s solve) refuse an exactly singular matrix; nudge the shift slightly.

Q11. Inverse iteration on a \(2000 \times 2000\) dense matrix takes 30 steps. Compare the work of calling solve(A - sigma * I, x) in every step with factoring once and reusing the factors.

Hint

LU costs about \(\tfrac23 n^3\); forward plus back substitution about \(2n^2\).

Answer

solve every step: \(30\cdot\tfrac23\cdot 2000^3 \approx 1.6\times10^{11}\) operations. Factor once: \(\tfrac23\cdot 2000^3 \approx 5.3\times10^9\) plus \(30\cdot 2\cdot 2000^2 = 2.4\times10^8\) for the solves, about 30 times less. (Rayleigh quotient iteration changes the shift every step and so must refactor every step; it makes up for it by needing only 2–3 steps.)

Q12. When would you write power iteration yourself instead of calling eigen()?

Hint

What does eigen() compute, and what does it cost in time and memory?

Answer

eigen() computes all eigenvalues with \(O(n^3)\) work and \(n^2\) memory: the right choice for any matrix you can hold dense (a 4 × 4 Leslie matrix, a 500-state chain). Power or inverse iteration is worth it when you need only one eigenpair of a large or sparse matrix, such as the growth rate of a size-structured model with thousands of classes, or the stationary distribution of a sparse chain with \(10^6\) states, where each step is one cheap (sparse) matrix–vector product or solve. And it is already what you do when you simulate the model forward.
