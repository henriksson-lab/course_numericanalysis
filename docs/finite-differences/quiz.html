Quiz: numerical differentiation

Short questions to test your understanding of numerical differentiation. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. Why does the central difference \(\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}\) have error \(O(h^2)\), while the forward difference has error \(O(h)\)?

Hint

Write the Taylor series of \(f(x+h)\) and \(f(x-h)\) and subtract them. Which terms cancel?

Answer

In \(f(x+h) - f(x-h)\) all even-power terms cancel, including \(\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''\). After dividing by \(2h\) the first leftover term is \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''\). In the forward difference nothing cancels the \(\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''\) term, which becomes \(\tfrac{h}{2}f''\) after dividing by \(h\). Symmetry buys one order.

Q2. You halve \(h\) in a second-order formula, and the error goes from \(4\times10^{-6}\) to \(1\times10^{-6}\). You halve it again, and the error goes to \(3\times10^{-6}\). What happened?

Hint

Which kind of error grows when \(h\) shrinks?

Answer

The first halving was in the truncation regime (error divided by \(4 = 2^2\)). The second step crossed the bottom of the V into the rounding regime, where the error grows like \(\varepsilon/h\) and jumps around. The best step lies between the two. Use the larger step, or extrapolate (Richardson) instead of shrinking \(h\).

Q3. Rule of thumb: the best step is about \(\varepsilon^{1/2}\) for a forward difference, \(\varepsilon^{1/3}\) for a central difference, \(\varepsilon^{1/4}\) for the second difference \((1, -2, 1)/h^2\). Explain the pattern in one sentence. Roughly how many correct digits does each give?

Hint

Balance truncation \(C h^p\) against rounding \(\varepsilon / h^k\) for the \(k\)-th derivative.

Answer

Setting \(h^p = \varepsilon/h^k\) gives \(h^* = \varepsilon^{1/(p+k)}\): \(p + k = 2, 3, 4\). The best error is \(\varepsilon^{p/(p+k)}\): about \(10^{-8}\) (8 digits), \(10^{-11}\) (11 digits) and \(10^{-8}\) (8 digits). A higher-order formula allows a larger step and keeps more digits; a higher derivative loses more.

Q4. A colleague computes the second derivative of a log-likelihood with (f(x + h) - 2 * f(x) + f(x - h)) / h^2 and h <- 1e-8, “because that is the standard step”. What will they get?

Hint

How large is the rounding error of the numerator, and what do you divide it by?

Answer

Garbage. The numerator is a difference of three nearly equal numbers, with rounding error about \(4\varepsilon|f| \approx 10^{-15}|f|\); dividing by \(h^2 = 10^{-16}\) gives an error of order \(10|f|\). In the lecture, the curvature of the OD curve came out as \(-0.56\) instead of \(+0.059\): wrong size and wrong sign. Use \(h \approx \varepsilon^{1/4}\max(|x|, 1) \approx 10^{-4}\max(|x|,1)\), or numDeriv::hessian.

Q5. \(D(0.1) = 1.0040\) and \(D(0.05) = 1.0010\) are central-difference estimates of a derivative. Give a better estimate, and say what assumption it relies on.

Hint

The central difference has error \(c_2 h^2 + O(h^4)\).

Answer

Richardson: \(\tfrac{4\cdot1.0010 - 1.0040}{3} = \tfrac{4.0040 - 1.0040}{3} = 1.0000\). It assumes the error really behaves like \(c_2h^2\) at these steps: a smooth function, both steps in the truncation regime, no measurement noise. (Check: the error ratio should be about 4. Here \(D(0.1) - 1 = 0.004\) and \(D(0.05) - 1 = 0.001\): ratio 4.)

Q6. A plate reader measures OD every 10 minutes with noise sd 0.005. Your new reader measures every 1 minute with the same noise. You compute the growth rate as the central difference of neighbouring points. Does the growth rate get more or less accurate, and by what factor?

Hint

The noise in \((y_{i+1} - y_{i-1})/(2\Delta t)\) has sd \(\sigma/(\sqrt2\,\Delta t)\).

Answer

Less accurate, by a factor of 10: the noise sd goes from \(0.021\) to \(0.21\) OD/h, about the size of the growth rate itself. Differentiation amplifies noise by \(1/\Delta t\). The extra data only help if you use them to average: a wider stencil, a local fit over many points, or a smoothing spline.

Q7. Why does smoothing the data before differentiating help, and what does it cost? Name one check that tells you whether your smoothing is reasonable.

Hint

Think of the V again: what plays the role of rounding, and what the role of truncation?

Answer

Smoothing averages many measurements, so the noise in the derivative falls (the “rounding” side of the V). The cost is bias (the “truncation” side): peaks become lower and wider, and the ends of the curve are less reliable. Check: compute the derivative with two different smoothing levels (stencil widths, spline df) and plot both; features that appear in both are real, features that move or vanish are noise. And look for impossible values, such as negative growth rates in mid-exponential phase.

Q8. You fit a model with 50 parameters by maximum likelihood; one evaluation of the log-likelihood takes 1 second. How long does one gradient take by forward differences, by central differences, and by reverse-mode automatic differentiation?

Hint

One difference per parameter. Reverse mode costs a small multiple of one evaluation, whatever the number of parameters.

Answer

Forward: \(51\) evaluations, about 51 s. Central: 100 s. Reverse-mode AD: a few evaluations’ worth, roughly 2–4 s. With hundreds of optimiser iterations, this is the difference between minutes and hours, and why Stan, TMB, JAX and PyTorch use AD.

Q9. optim(par, fn, method = "BFGS") without a gradient returns. What is the cheapest check that it really stopped at a maximum (or minimum), and why might it not have?

Hint

What should the gradient be at an optimum, and how does optim compute it?

Answer

Evaluate an accurate gradient at the returned point, e.g. numDeriv::grad(fn, fit$par), and check that it is small compared with the gradient at the start. optim uses central differences with a fixed absolute step ndeps = 1e-3; for parameters much smaller than 1 that step is a large fraction of the parameter, the gradient has a large truncation error, and BFGS stops at the wrong place. Fix: supply gr, or set parscale to the parameter sizes.

Q10. You wrote an analytic gradient for your model by hand. How do you test it, and what result tells you it has a bug?

Hint

Compare it with something that cannot have the same bug.

Answer

Compare it, component by component, with a central-difference gradient (or numDeriv::grad) at a few random parameter values. Correct code agrees to a relative difference of about \(10^{-7}\) to \(10^{-10}\). A component that differs by order 1 (e.g. 85 % in the exercise, where a factor \(t\) was missing) points at the wrong partial derivative. Do not test only at the optimum, where both gradients are near zero.

Q11. What is the complex-step derivative \(\operatorname{Im} f(x + ih)/h\), why can it use \(h = 10^{-20}\), and when can you not use it?

Hint

Where did the rounding error of the forward difference come from?

Answer

Taylor: \(f(x + ih) = f(x) + ihf'(x) - \tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x) + \dots\), so the imaginary part divided by \(h\) is \(f'(x) + O(h^2)\). There is no subtraction of nearly equal numbers, so no cancellation, and a tiny \(h\) makes the truncation error vanish: the result is exact to rounding. It only works if every operation in \(f\) accepts complex numbers; abs, max, comparisons, and compiled solvers (like an ODE solver) break it.
