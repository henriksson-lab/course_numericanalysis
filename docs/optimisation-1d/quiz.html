Quiz: optimisation in one dimension

Short questions to test your understanding of one-dimensional minimisation: brackets, golden section, Brent, Newton, the flat bottom, and local versus global minima. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. You find the maximum-likelihood estimate by solving score \(= 0\) with a root finder. Name one thing that can go wrong which cannot happen when you maximise the log-likelihood directly.

Hint

Which kinds of points have a zero derivative?

Answer

A root of the score is any stationary point: a maximum of the likelihood, but also a minimum or a flat inflection point. A root finder cannot tell them apart. A maximiser compares likelihood values and only accepts better ones, so it cannot end at a minimum of the likelihood. (Both can still end at a local maximum.)

Q2. For root finding, two points with \(f(a) < 0 < f(b)\) are a bracket. Why does a bracket for a minimum need three points?

Hint

Suppose \(f(a) > f(b)\). Where could the minimum be?

Answer

Two values only tell you which one is lower, not whether the function turns upwards again between them: with \(f(a) > f(b)\) the minimum may lie between \(a\) and \(b\), or beyond \(b\). A third point \(c\) between them with \(f(c) < f(a)\) and \(f(c) < f(b)\) proves that the function goes down and then up again, so a (local) minimum lies in \((a, b)\).

Q3. Golden-section search starts on an interval of width 2. What is the width after 10 function evaluations? How many evaluations does it take to reach \(10^{-6}\)?

Hint

The first two evaluations give one comparison; every further evaluation multiplies the width by 0.618.

Answer

After \(m\) evaluations the width is \(2 \cdot 0.618^{m-1}\): after 10 evaluations \(2\cdot0.618^9 = 0.026\). For \(10^{-6}\): \(m - 1 \ge \ln(5\times10^{-7})/\ln 0.618 = 30.2\), so \(m = 32\) evaluations. Linear convergence: about 0.21 decimal digits per evaluation.

Q4. Why is golden-section search slower than bisection (0.618 versus 0.5 per evaluation), when both just shrink an interval?

Hint

How much information is in one new evaluation for each method?

Answer

In bisection one new value (its sign) decides which half to keep. In golden section a decision needs a comparison of two interior values; one of them is reused from the previous step, but the two points cannot both be at the midpoint, so the part thrown away is only 38% of the interval. That is the price for not needing the derivative: bisection on \(f'\) is faster, but needs \(f'\).

Q5. In one sentence each: what does Brent’s method take from golden section, and what from parabolic interpolation?

Hint

Safety and speed.

Answer

From golden section: the bracket and the guaranteed shrinking, used whenever a parabolic step is unsafe, so the method cannot run away. From parabolic interpolation: fast (superlinear, order about 1.32) steps to the vertex of the parabola through the three best points, used when they land inside the bracket and keep shrinking. R: optimize; Python: minimize_scalar; Rust: BrentOpt.

Q6. optimize(f, c(0, 10)) returns 0.3754002 where the true minimum is 0.3753873. Is the method broken?

Hint

Look up the default value of tol.

Answer

No. The default tol is \(\varepsilon^{1/4} \approx 1.2\times10^{-4}\), an absolute tolerance, and the error (\(1.3\times10^{-5}\)) is within it. Set tol yourself (e.g. tol = 1e-10); it costs only a few more evaluations. The same holds for uniroot.

Q7. Why can a minimiser that only compares values of \(f\) locate a minimum only to about \(\sqrt{\varepsilon} \approx 10^{-8}\) (relative), while a root of \(f'\) can be found to about \(10^{-16}\)?

Hint

Taylor-expand \(f\) around \(x^*\), where \(f'(x^*) = 0\).

Answer

\(f(x^* + \delta) \approx f(x^*) + \tfrac12 f''\delta^2\): the change is quadratic in \(\delta\). Once \(\tfrac12 f''\delta^2\) is below the rounding level \(\varepsilon|f(x^*)|\), all points within \(\delta \sim \sqrt{\varepsilon}\) (times \(\sqrt{2|f|/f''}\)) have the same computed \(f\). The derivative changes linearly, \(f'(x^* + \delta) \approx f''\delta\), so its sign change is visible down to \(\delta \sim \varepsilon\). If you need more than about 8 digits, find the root of \(f'\).

Q8. Newton for minimisation from \(x_0\) gives \(x_1\) with \(f(x_1) > f(x_0)\), and \(f''(x_0) < 0\). What happened, and what should a careful implementation do?

Hint

Draw the local parabola \(f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac12 f''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2\).

Answer

With \(f''(x_0) < 0\) the local parabola opens downwards; its vertex is a maximum, and the Newton step \(-f'/f''\) points uphill. Newton (a root finder for \(f'\)) heads for a stationary point, not necessarily a minimum. A careful implementation checks \(f'' > 0\) before taking the step and \(f(x_{k+1}) < f(x_k)\) after it; if a check fails it takes a different step (a bracketing step, or a shorter step downhill) instead.

Q9. \(f\) is convex on \((0, \infty)\). Which of these are then guaranteed? (i) every local minimum is the global minimum; (ii) Newton for minimisation converges from every start; (iii) golden section on any bracket converges to the minimum.

Hint

Think of \(f(x) = x - 2\ln x\) and Newton from \(x_0 = 5\).

Answer
  1. Yes. (iii) Yes: a convex function is unimodal on any bracket. (ii) No: convexity makes each Newton step point downhill, but the step can be far too long. For \(x - 2\ln x\), Newton from \(x_0 \ge 4\) jumps to \(x \le 0\), outside the domain. A step-length check (does \(f\) decrease?) is still needed.

Q10. You fit the period of a circadian rhythm by minimising \(\text{RSS}(T)\) with optimize on \([10, 40]\) h and get \(T = 32.4\) h. On \([15, 35]\) h you get \(24.6\) h. Which do you report, and what would you do to be sure?

Hint

Both are local minima. Which has the smaller objective?

Answer

Compare the objective: RSS is 1.91 at 24.6 h and 10.23 at 32.4 h (about the RSS of no rhythm at all), so 24.6 h is the better fit and the one to report. To be sure: evaluate RSS on a grid finer than the narrowest dip (here 0.25 h), plot it, and refine the best grid point with optimize on a small interval around it. optimize is a local method: on \([10, 40]\) its first golden-section points both fell on the flat part, and it never saw the dip.

Q11. optimize(f, c(0.5, 2)) returns 0.5000000. What does this tell you?

Hint

Where is the answer, relative to the interval?

Answer

The answer is (within tol) at the end of the interval. Almost always this means the minimum lies outside the interval and the method crept towards the nearest end; it gives no warning. Plot \(f\), widen the interval and run again. (If the parameter really is bounded, e.g. a rate \(\ge 0\), an optimum on the boundary is a constrained problem: Constraints and linear programming.)
