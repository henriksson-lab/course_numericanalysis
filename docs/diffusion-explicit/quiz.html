Quiz: the diffusion (heat) equation by finite differences

Short questions to test your understanding of the diffusion equation, the FTCS scheme, its stability and its boundary conditions. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. For a concentration \(c(x, t)\) in a channel, what do \(\partial c/\partial x\) and \(\partial c/\partial t\) mean, in words a cell biologist would use?

Hint

Hold one variable fixed and look at the slope along the other.

Answer

\(\partial c/\partial x\): at a fixed moment, how steeply the concentration changes with position, the gradient a cell feels across its body. \(\partial c/\partial t\): at a fixed place, how fast the concentration there rises or falls over time, what one stationary cell sees changing.

Q2. Fick’s law says \(J = -D\,\partial c/\partial x\). Why the minus sign, and how does a mass balance turn it into \(\partial c/\partial t = D\,\partial^2 c/\partial x^2\)?

Hint

Which way do molecules move on a profile that falls to the right? For the balance: rate in − rate out of a thin slice.

Answer

Molecules move from high to low concentration: where \(\partial c/\partial x < 0\) the flux is to the right (\(J > 0\)). In a slice \([x, x + \Delta x]\) the amount changes by what flows in minus what flows out, \(\partial c/\partial t\,\Delta x = J(x) - J(x + \Delta x)\). Dividing by \(\Delta x\) gives \(\partial c/\partial t = -\partial J/\partial x = D\,\partial^2 c/\partial x^2\).

Q3. A chemokine with \(D = 10^{-10}\) m²/s diffuses across a 2 mm chamber. Estimate the diffusion time scale. How does it change if the chamber is made 1 mm long?

Hint

Convert \(D\) to µm²/s and compute \(L^2/D\).

Answer

\(D = 100\) µm²/s, \(L^2/D = (2000)^2/100 = 40\,000\) s, about 11 hours (the linear gradient within 5 % after about \(0.26\,L^2/D \approx 2.9\) h). Halving \(L\) divides the time by 4: \(10\,000\) s, about 2.8 hours (\(\approx 43\) min to the gradient).

Q4. Write the FTCS update and say which ideas from earlier units it combines.

Hint

One stencil in space, one ODE method in time.

Answer

\(c_i^{n+1} = c_i^n + r\,(c_{i-1}^n - 2c_i^n + c_{i+1}^n)\) with \(r = D\Delta t/\Delta x^2\). The bracket divided by \(\Delta x^2\) is the second-difference stencil \((1, -2, 1)/\Delta x^2\) from Numerical differentiation; stepping forward with the slope at the start of the step is Euler’s method from Initial value problems and Euler’s method.

Q5. One FTCS step with \(r = 0.25\) on the values \((c_{i-1}, c_i, c_{i+1}) = (0.6, 0.2, 0.4)\): what is the new \(c_i\)? Is it inside the range of the old values?

Hint

Rewrite the update as \((1 - 2r)c_i + r\,c_{i-1} + r\,c_{i+1}\).

Answer

\(0.2 + 0.25(0.6 - 0.4 + 0.4) = 0.2 + 0.15 = 0.35\). As weights: \(0.5 \cdot 0.2 + 0.25 \cdot 0.6 + 0.25 \cdot 0.4 = 0.35\). A weighted average with non-negative weights, so it lies between 0.2 and 0.6. This holds whenever \(r \le 1/2\).

Q6. Where does the limit \(r \le 1/2\) come from? Give the short argument with the zigzag \(c_i = (-1)^i\).

Hint

Compute the second difference of the zigzag, then the factor by which one step multiplies it.

Answer

For the zigzag \(c_{i-1} - 2c_i + c_{i+1} = -4c_i\), so one step multiplies it by \(1 - 4r\). It does not grow only if \(|1 - 4r| \le 1\), i.e. \(r \le 1/2\). This is Euler’s condition \(|1 - kh| \le 1\) with the decay rate \(k = 4D/\Delta x^2\) of the zigzag, the fastest mode on the grid (the most negative eigenvalue of the second-difference matrix times \(D/\Delta x^2\)).

Q7. A student simulates a small molecule (\(D = 1000\) µm²/s) on a grid with \(\Delta x = 10\) µm and \(\Delta t = 0.1\) s. What will she see, and how could she tell without an exact solution?

Hint

Compute \(r\).

Answer

\(r = 1000 \cdot 0.1/100 = 1\), twice the limit. A point-to-point zigzag appears where the profile is steepest and grows by a factor up to 3 per step, so the values soon reach absurd sizes. The check: concentrations must stay between the smallest and largest initial/boundary values (here 0 and 1). Values outside that range, or negative values, mean the step is too large (or there is a bug). The fix with FTCS is \(\Delta t \le 0.05\) s.

Q8. Halving \(\Delta x\) in an FTCS simulation with \(r\) kept fixed: by what factor does the error change, and by what factor the work? Why?

Hint

\(\Delta t = r\,\Delta x^2/D\). Count points and steps.

Answer

The error falls by about 4: the stencil is \(O(\Delta x^2)\), and Euler’s \(O(\Delta t)\) is also \(O(\Delta x^2)\) when \(\Delta t \propto \Delta x^2\). The work grows by 8: twice as many points and four times as many steps. Accuracy is cheap in space but the stability limit makes it expensive in time; implicit schemes (next unit) remove the limit.

Q9. The far end of a channel is a closed wall instead of a sink reservoir. Which boundary condition replaces \(c(L, t) = 0\), how is it put into FTCS, and what is the steady state?

Hint

Nothing crosses a wall. Invent a point just outside it.

Answer

No flux: \(J = 0\), so \(\partial c/\partial x = 0\) at \(x = L\). With a ghost point \(c_{N+1} = c_{N-1}\) the wall update is \(c_N^{n+1} = c_N^n + 2r\,(c_{N-1}^n - c_N^n)\). The steady state is \(c = 1\) everywhere (the source value): the channel fills up and there is no lasting gradient. A chemotaxis chamber needs a sink.

Q10. In the lecture, four runs (two molecules, two channel lengths) all needed exactly 1611 FTCS steps to reach the 5 % criterion. Why is that not a coincidence, and what does it tell you about the biology?

Hint

What do the runs have in common, and what is the only thing that differs?

Answer

All runs used \(N = 50\) and \(r = 0.4\), so they did exactly the same arithmetic on the same numbers; only the physical length of one step, \(\Delta t = r\,\Delta x^2/D = r L^2/(N^2 D)\), differed. In the variables \(x/L\) and \(tD/L^2\) the diffusion equation has no parameters left, so every chamber is a rescaled copy of one problem: the gradient is ready at \(t \approx 0.26\,L^2/D\), whatever the molecule or the length. Doubling \(L\) quadruples the wait; a ten times slower molecule waits ten times longer.

Q11. The grid ODEs \(\mathbf c' = \tfrac{D}{\Delta x^2}A\mathbf c + \mathbf b\) have decay rates from about \(0.04\) per s to \(40\) per s (\(N = 50\)). Which rate matters for the biology, which one limits FTCS, and what is this situation called?

Hint

Look at the shapes of the slowest and fastest eigenvectors.

Answer

The slowest rate (\(\approx \pi^2 D/L^2\), a half-sine mode, time constant 25 s) sets how fast the gradient settles: that is the biology. The fastest (the zigzag, \(\approx 4D/\Delta x^2\)) decays in hundredths of a second and has no biological meaning, yet it limits Euler’s step to \(\Delta t \le 2/40 = 0.05\) s. A step set by stability rather than accuracy, with rates a thousand times apart, is stiffness (Stiffness and implicit methods), and it gets worse like \(N^2\) as the grid is refined.
