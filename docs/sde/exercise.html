Exercise: stochastic differential equations

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • explain why Brownian increments have standard deviation \(\sqrt h\), and estimate a diffusion coefficient from particle tracks with the mean squared displacement (MSD),
  • carry out Euler–Maruyama (EM) and Milstein steps by hand and hand-write both in code,
  • measure the strong (path) and weak (average) order of a method on a log–log plot, with the same Brownian path at every step size, and recognise the fresh-noise bug,
  • explain the Itô correction \(-\sigma^2/2\): why the mean of a population can grow while the typical population dies out,
  • detect negative values, variance errors and mean-square instability, and fix the last with drift-implicit EM,
  • solve the Fokker–Planck equation of a small SDE with the advection–diffusion scheme and compare it with a histogram of paths.

The by-hand part uses pen, paper and a pocket calculator (for \(\sqrt{\ }\), exp and log). The code part needs only normal random numbers; no SDE package is used.

Background

Facts used below:

  1. Brownian motion \(W(t)\): \(W(0) = 0\), and the increment over a step \(h\), \(\Delta W = W(t + h) - W(t)\), is \(N(0, h)\) and independent of everything before \(t\). Variances of independent increments add. In code: \(\Delta W = \sqrt h\, Z\) with \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\).
  2. Diffusing particle. Each coordinate moves by \(\sqrt{2D\,\Delta t}\, Z\) per frame. Mean squared displacement \(\text{MSD}(t) = 2dDt\) in \(d\) dimensions. Stokes–Einstein for a sphere of radius \(a\) in a liquid of viscosity \(\eta\): \(D = k_BT/(6\pi\eta a)\); a bead of radius 0.5 µm in water at 20 °C has \(D \approx 0.43\) µm²/s.
  3. SDE and Euler–Maruyama. \(dX = f(X)\,dt + g(X)\,dW\): \[X_{n+1} = X_n + f(X_n)\,h + g(X_n)\,\Delta W_n.\] Milstein adds \(\tfrac12\,g(X_n)\,g'(X_n)\,\big((\Delta W_n)^2 - h\big)\). With additive noise (\(g\) constant) the two are the same.
  4. Test problems with exact solutions.
    • Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU), bead in an optical trap: \(dX = -\theta X\,dt + \sigma\,dW\). Mean \(x_0 e^{-\theta t}\), variance \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t})\) (plus \(s_0^2 e^{-2\theta t}\) if \(X(0)\) has variance \(s_0^2\)), stationary variance \(\sigma^2/(2\theta)\). Trap: \(\theta = 1\) per ms, \(D = \sigma^2/2 = 430\) nm²/ms (positions in nm, time in ms).
    • Geometric Brownian motion (GBM), a population with a noisy growth rate: \(dN = rN\,dt + \sigma N\,dW\), \(N(T) = N_0\exp\!\big((r - \sigma^2/2)T + \sigma W(T)\big)\). Mean \(N_0 e^{rT}\), median \(N_0 e^{(r - \sigma^2/2)T}\).
  5. Errors. Strong: \(\mathrm E\,|X_n - X(T)|\) on the same Brownian path. Weak: \(|\mathrm E\,X_n - \mathrm E\,X(T)|\). For multiplicative noise EM has strong order ½ and weak order 1; Milstein strong order 1.
  6. Fokker–Planck. The density \(p(x, t)\) of many paths solves \[\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big(f\,p - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(\tfrac{g^2}{2}\,p\big)\Big),\] advection–diffusion with velocity \(u = f\) and \(D = g^2/2\). For constant \(D\), the explicit flux scheme on cells of width \(\Delta x\): \[F_{i+1/2} = u_{i+1/2}\,\frac{p_i + p_{i+1}}{2} - D\,\frac{p_{i+1} - p_i}{\Delta x}, \qquad p_i^{n+1} = p_i^n - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\big(F_{i+1/2} - F_{i-1/2}\big).\]

The three languages use different random number generators (R: Mersenne Twister; numpy: PCG64; Rust: ChaCha12), so the same seed gives different paths: compare statistics between languages, never single paths.

// rust/examples/sde_background.rs  —  cargo run --example sde_background
// Background: Euler-Maruyama for a bead in an optical trap (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck), 2000 beads.
use rand::{rngs::StdRng, RngExt, SeedableRng};
use rand_distr::StandardNormal;

fn main() {
    let (theta, d) = (1.0f64, 430.0f64); // trap: per ms, nm^2/ms
    let sigma = (2.0 * d).sqrt(); // nm/sqrt(ms)
    let mut rng = StdRng::seed_from_u64(1);
    let h = 0.01;
    let mut x = vec![60.0f64; 2000]; // 2000 beads, all at 60 nm
    for _ in 0..100 {
        // 100 EM steps to t = 1 ms
        for xi in x.iter_mut() {
            let z: f64 = rng.sample(StandardNormal);
            *xi += -theta * *xi * h + sigma * h.sqrt() * z;
        }
    }
    let n = x.len() as f64;
    let mean = x.iter().sum::<f64>() / n;
    let var = x.iter().map(|v| (v - mean).powi(2)).sum::<f64>() / (n - 1.0);
    println!("mean {:.4}  var {:.4}", mean, var);
    println!("exact mean {:.4}  var {:.4}", 60.0 * (-theta).exp(), d / theta * (1.0 - (-2.0 * theta).exp()));
}

// Output:
// mean 22.9555  var 380.1516
// exact mean 22.0728  var 371.8058

The mean has a standard error of \(\sqrt{372/2000} = 0.43\) nm and the variance one of about \(372\sqrt{2/2000} = 12\) nm²; all three languages agree with the exact values within about 2 standard errors. EM with \(\theta h = 0.01\) itself makes the variance about 0.7% too large (374.3 instead of 371.8; compare B3).

By hand

B1. Brownian increments. Brownian motion on \([0, 1]\) is built from four increments of \(h = 0.25\). (a) What are the distribution and standard deviation of each increment? Of \(W(1)\)? Of \(W(0.5)\), and of \(W(1) - W(0.5)\)? (b) A random walk takes \(1/h\) steps of \(\pm h\) (instead of \(\pm\sqrt h\)). What is the variance of its position at \(t = 1\) for \(h = 0.25\) and \(h = 0.01\)? What happens as \(h \to 0\)? (c) The “slope” of a Brownian path over one step is \(\Delta W/h\). Using \(\mathrm E\,|Z| = 0.80\), what is its typical size \(\mathrm E\,|\Delta W|/h\) for \(h = 0.01\) and \(h = 10^{-4}\)? What does this say about \(dW/dt\)? (d) A bead moves by \(\sqrt{2D\,\Delta t}\,Z\) in \(x\) per frame. Show that after \(n\) frames (\(t = n\Delta t\)) the variance of \(x\) is \(2Dt\), and that the 2D MSD is \(4Dt\).

B2. One step of EM and of Milstein. (a) Bead in the trap: \(\theta = 1\) per ms, \(\sigma = 30\) nm/\(\sqrt{\text{ms}}\), \(x_0 = 40\) nm, \(h = 0.01\) ms. Do two EM steps with \(Z_0 = -1.2\), \(Z_1 = 0.5\). Compare the size of the drift step and of the kick. (b) Population: \(dN = 0.8\,N\,dt + 0.5\,N\,dW\), \(N_0 = 100\), \(h = 0.04\) h, \(Z_0 = 1.5\) (so \(\Delta W = 0.3\)). Do one EM step and one Milstein step. (c) The exact solution on the same path is \(N = 100\,\exp\!\big((0.8 - 0.125)\,0.04 + 0.5 \cdot 0.3\big)\). Compute it and the errors of (b). Why is the Milstein term the same for the trap with any \(Z\)?

B3. Stationary variance of the OU recursion. EM for the trap is \(x_{n+1} = (1 - \theta h)\,x_n + \sigma\sqrt h\,Z_n\). (a) For which \(h\) does the mean \(\mathrm E\,x_n\) decay to 0? (Take the expectation of the recursion.) (b) Let \(v = \mathrm{Var}\,x_n\) in the long run. Using that \(x_n\) and \(Z_n\) are independent, show \(v = (1 - \theta h)^2 v + \sigma^2 h\) and solve: \(v = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\theta(2 - \theta h)}\). (c) With \(\theta = 1\), \(\sigma^2 = 860\): compute \(v\) for \(h = 0.1, 1, 1.5\) and compare with the exact \(\sigma^2/(2\theta) = 430\). What is the relative error for small \(h\)? (d) The exact recursion is \(x_{n+1} = e^{-\theta h}x_n + b\,Z_n\) with \(b^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta h})\). Repeat (b) for it.

B4. Mean up, median down. A population follows \(dN = rN\,dt + \sigma N\,dW\) with \(r = 0.1\) per year, \(\sigma = 0.6\) per \(\sqrt{\text{year}}\), \(N_0 = 1000\). (a) Compute the mean and the median after 10 and 50 years. (b) After how many years has the median halved? (c) For which \(\sigma\) does the median stay constant? Is \(\sigma = 0.4\) below or above that? (d) A cartoon with \(h = 1\) year: each year multiplies \(N\) by \(1 + r + \sigma Z\), and suppose \(Z = +1\) and \(Z = -1\) come in turn. Compute the two factors, their mean, and the factor over two years. Which one describes a single population?

B5. Mean-square stability. Drug clearance with a noisy rate: \(dC = -kC\,dt + \mu C\,dW\), \(k = 0.8\) per hour, \(\mu = 1\) per \(\sqrt{\text{hour}}\). (a) EM: \(C_{n+1} = C_n(1 - kh + \mu\,\Delta W_n)\). Using \(\mathrm E\,\Delta W = 0\), \(\mathrm E\,\Delta W^2 = h\) and independence, show \(\mathrm E\,C_{n+1}^2 = \big((1 - kh)^2 + \mu^2 h\big)\,\mathrm E\,C_n^2\). (b) Compute this factor for \(h = 0.5, 1, 2\). What does Euler for the ODE \(C' = -0.8\,C\) do at these steps? (c) For which \(h\) is EM mean-square stable? (d) Drift-implicit EM: \(C_{n+1} = C_n - khC_{n+1} + \mu C_n\,\Delta W_n\). Solve for \(C_{n+1}\), find its mean-square factor and show that it is below 1 for every \(h > 0\).

B6. \(D\) from an MSD by hand. A 2D bead track gives MSD values 0.18, 0.33 and 0.52 µm² at lags 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 s. (a) Fit a line through the origin, \(\text{MSD} = b\,t\): the least-squares slope is \(b = \sum t_k m_k / \sum t_k^2\). Compute \(b\) and \(D\). (b) By Stokes–Einstein, what is the bead radius if a bead of radius 0.5 µm has \(D = 0.43\) µm²/s in the same liquid? (c) The same bead in a channel with flow \(u = 5\) µm/s along \(x\). By how much does the flow change the 2D MSD at lags 0.1 and 0.3 s (\(+u^2t^2\))? What would you fit instead?

In code

C1. \(D\) from particle tracks. Simulate 2D tracks of a bead (\(D = 0.43\) µm²/s, 10 frames per second) as cumulative sums of normal steps with sd \(\sqrt{2D\Delta t}\) per coordinate (cumsum; Python np.cumsum). (a) Write msd(track, K) (time-averaged MSD at lags \(1, \dots, K\)) and Dhat(track, K) (slope of a line through the origin, divided by \(2d\)). Estimate \(D\) from one track of 100 frames with \(K = 4\) and \(K = 50\). (b) Over 1000 tracks: mean and sd of \(\hat D\) for 100 frames with \(K = 4\) and \(K = 50\), and for 1000 frames with \(K = 4\). (c) Add a flow \(u = 5\) µm/s along \(x\). Compare the mean of \(\hat D\) from both coordinates with that from \(y\) only.

C2. Strong and weak order of EM. GBM with \(r = 0.8\), \(\sigma = 0.5\), \(N_0 = 1\), \(T = 1\). Draw \(M = 10\,000\) fine Brownian paths with \(2^{10}\) steps and compute the exact \(N(T)\) on each. For \(h = 2^{-10}, \dots, 2^{-2}\), run EM on every path with increments made by summing the fine ones (rowsum; Python dWf.reshape(nf // R, R, M).sum(axis=1)). (a) Print the strong error \(\text{mean}\,|N_n - N(T)|\) and the weak error \(|\text{mean}(N_n - N(T))|\) for each \(h\), and fit the slopes on a log–log scale (lm; Python np.polyfit). (b) For \(h = 1/4\), compare the plain mean of \(N_n\) with the exact \(e^{rT}\), and with twice the standard error of a mean of \(M\) values of \(N(T)\). Why could this comparison not show the weak error at \(h = 1/256\)?

C3. The fresh-noise bug. Repeat C2(a), but give each step size new normal increments of variance \(h\) instead of summing the fine ones. What happens to the strong error? Is the weak error still meaningful?

C4. The Itô surprise. GBM with \(r = 0.1\) per year, \(N_0 = 1000\), EM with \(h = 0.05\) year, 10 000 populations, 50 years. (a) With \(\sigma = 0.6\): the sample mean, median and fraction of populations below 10 after 10 and 50 years, against the exact values (mean, median, and \(P(N(t) < 10) = \Phi\big((\ln(10/1000) - (r - \sigma^2/2)t)/(\sigma\sqrt t)\big)\); pnorm, Python scipy.stats.norm.cdf). (b) Repeat with \(\sigma = 0.4\). (c) Why is the sample mean after 50 years so far from the exact mean, although the median is close?

C5. Milstein against EM. With the paths of C2, add the Milstein term for GBM (\(g = \sigma N\), \(g' = \sigma\)) and print the strong errors of both methods with their fitted slopes.

C6. Fokker–Planck against a histogram. The trap (\(\theta = 1\) per ms, \(D = 430\) nm²/ms) is moved at \(t = 0\) so that the beads start at \(N(60, 5^2)\) nm. (a) Solve the Fokker–Planck equation (Fact 6) on \(x \in [-100, 160]\) nm with \(\Delta x = 2\) nm, \(\Delta t = 0.002\) ms and zero flux at both ends, up to \(t = 1\) and \(t = 3\) ms. Print the mass \(\sum p\,\Delta x\), the mean and the variance. Check \(C + 2r \le 1\) and the cell Péclet number \(P = |u|\Delta x/D < 2\) first. (b) Replace the central average \((p_i + p_{i+1})/2\) in the drift flux by the upwind value (\(p_i\) if \(u > 0\), else \(p_{i+1}\)). How do the variances change, and why? (c) Run EM for 10 000 beads with \(h = 0.01\) ms and compare the sample mean and variance with (a) and with the exact OU values (Fact 4).

C7. Stable for the ODE, unstable for the SDE. For the noisy drug clearance of B5 (\(C_0 = 10\) mg/L, 10 000 patients, 48 h), run EM and drift-implicit EM with \(h = 0.5, 1, 2\) h, using the same increments for both. Print the mean-square factors of B5, the largest \(|C|\) over the patients at 48 h and, for EM, the sample mean of \(C^2\). Compare with the exact \(\mathrm E\,C(48)^2 = C_0^2\,e^{(-2k + \mu^2)\,48}\).

Explore

The demo simulates the trap (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck, \(dX = \theta(\mu - X)\,dt + \sigma\,dW\) with \(\mu = 0\), \(x_0 = 3\), up to \(t = 5\)) or geometric Brownian motion (\(dX = rX\,dt + \sigma X\,dW\), up to \(t = 1\)) in your browser. Panel (a) shows 20 numerical paths and, for path 1, the exact solution driven by the same Brownian path; the gap is the strong error. All step sizes are built by summing increments of one fine Brownian path (\(2^{16}\) steps), so same path, finer step halves \(h\) the right way (C2), and new noise draws a new path. Panel (b) is the histogram of many paths at the chosen time with the exact density on top: the solution of the Fokker–Planck equation (C6). The readout gives the mean over the 20 paths of each path’s largest error, and its ratio from one step size to the next.

E1. (relaxation) Choose the preset OU relaxes (\(\theta = 1\), \(\sigma = 1\)) and press ▶ play. How do the mean and variance of the histogram change with time? What are the limits, by the formulas of Fact 4?

Answer E1

The mean decays like \(3e^{-t}\) and the variance grows like \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t})\) towards the stationary \(\sigma^2/(2\theta) = 0.5\), with mean 0. By \(t = 5\) the histogram sits on the exact normal \(N(0, 0.5)\), up to Monte Carlo noise of a few per cent.

E2. (weak error you can see) Stay with OU, set the time to the end and move the step slider to its largest value, \(h = 1.25\). Compare the variance in the readout with the exact one and with B3. Then press same path, finer step twice.

Answer E2

At \(h = 1.25\) (\(\theta h = 1.25\)) the EM variance tends to \(\sigma^2/(\theta(2 - \theta h)) = 1/0.75 = 1.33\), not 0.5: the histogram is far too wide. At \(h = 0.625\) it is \(1/1.375 = 0.73\), at \(h = 0.3125\) \(1/1.6875 = 0.59\). The relative excess \(\theta h/(2 - \theta h)\) is about \(\theta h/2\): the error in the distribution shrinks like \(h\), weak order 1 (B3(c)).

E3. (strong order) With OU, start at the coarsest step and press same path, finer step several times; read the ratios ÷ in the second readout line. Then choose the preset GBM: EM vs Milstein, do the same with Euler–Maruyama and with Milstein. Finally press new noise: what changes?

Answer E3

For OU the error falls by about 2 per halving: with additive noise (\(g\) constant) EM is Milstein, strong order 1. For GBM with EM it falls by only about \(\sqrt 2 \approx 1.4\) (strong order ½, between 1.1 and 1.6 for 20 paths); with Milstein by about 2 (order 1), as in C2 and C5. New noise changes the single numbers but not the pattern; with only 20 paths the ratios scatter, which is why C2 averages over 10 000.

E4. (mean-square stability) Choose the preset OU, θh = 2.5 (unstable) (\(\theta = 8\), \(h = 0.3125\)). What happens to the paths? What is the largest stable step, and what happens after one press of same path, finer step?

Answer E4

Each step multiplies the deviation by \(1 - \theta h = -1.5\) and adds noise, so the paths flip sign and grow without bound; the variance recursion of B3 has factor \((1 - \theta h)^2 = 2.25 > 1\). The limit is \(\theta h < 2\), \(h < 0.25\). After halving, \(h = 0.156\) and \(\theta h = 1.25\): the paths stay bounded (they still overshoot, factor \(-0.25\) per step), and the long-run variance is \(1/(8 \cdot 0.75) = 0.17\) against the exact \(1/16 = 0.0625\). Stable is not the same as accurate.

Hints

Hint 1 — increments and variances (B1, B3, B5)

For independent \(X\), \(Y\): \(\mathrm{Var}(X + Y) = \mathrm{Var}\,X + \mathrm{Var}\,Y\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(aX) = a^2\,\mathrm{Var}\,X\). The increment \(\Delta W_n\) is independent of \(x_n\), because \(x_n\) depends only on earlier increments. In a long run the variance stops changing: set \(\mathrm{Var}\,x_{n+1} = \mathrm{Var}\,x_n = v\). For B5, expand \((1 - kh + \mu\Delta W)^2\) and take expectations term by term.

Hint 2 — Milstein and the exact GBM (B2, C5)

For \(g(N) = \sigma N\): \(g\,g' = \sigma N \cdot \sigma = \sigma^2 N\), so the Milstein term is \(\frac12\sigma^2 N\big((\Delta W)^2 - h\big)\). For the trap \(g = \sigma\) is constant, so \(g' = 0\). The exact GBM solution on a path needs \(W(T)\), the sum of all increments of that path.

Hint 3 — MSD and least squares (B6, C1)

For lag \(k\) frames, average \(|\mathbf r_{i+k} - \mathbf r_i|^2\) over all \(i\). A line through the origin minimises \(\sum (m_k - b\,t_k)^2\), giving \(b = \sum t_k m_k/\sum t_k^2\); in R lm(m ~ 0 + t), in Python compute the formula directly or use np.linalg.lstsq with one column. Normal numbers: R rnorm; Python rng.normal / rng.standard_normal with rng = np.random.default_rng(1); Rust rng.sample(StandardNormal) or rand_distr::Normal::new(mean, sd) from rand_distr, with StdRng::seed_from_u64(1) from rand.

Hint 4 — same path at every step size (C2, C3, C5)

Store the fine increments as a matrix with one row per fine step and one column per path. For a coarse step of \(R\) fine steps, the coarse increments are sums of \(R\) consecutive rows: in R rowsum(dWf, rep(1:(nf / R), each = R)), in Python dWf.reshape(nf // R, R, M).sum(axis=1), in Rust dwf.chunks(R) per path. Loop over time steps and update all paths at once (vectorised) in R and Python. A slope is the coefficient of \(\log h\) in a straight-line fit of \(\log(\text{error})\).

Hint 5 — heavy tails and medians (C4)

The median is median in R and np.median in Python. The standard deviation of \(N(t)\) for GBM is \(N_0e^{rt}\sqrt{e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1}\); compare it with the mean, and ask how many of 10 000 populations account for most of the total.

Hint 6 — the Fokker–Planck scheme (C6)

Put the velocity at the faces \(x_{i+1/2} = x_i + \Delta x/2\): \(u = -\theta x_{i+1/2}\). Compute all fluxes from the old \(p\) first, then update (diff; Python np.diff). Zero flux at the ends: pad the flux vector with a 0 on each side, so that the update of cell \(i\) uses \(F_{i+1/2} - F_{i-1/2}\) for all cells. Then the total mass stays exactly 1, a cheap check. The start density is dnorm(x, 60, 5). Upwind adds a numerical diffusion \(\approx |u|\Delta x/2\) (Flow plus diffusion).

Hint 7 — mean-square stability in code (C7)

Draw one vector of increments per step and use it for both methods, so that the comparison is fair. The sample mean of \(C^2\) over 10 000 patients is dominated by the few largest values; compare it with the factor\(^{n}\) prediction only for the unstable case, where it is large. Print large and small numbers in scientific notation (%.1e).

B1. (a) Each increment is \(N(0, 0.25)\), sd 0.5. \(W(1)\) is the sum of four: variance 1, sd 1. \(W(0.5)\): two increments, variance 0.5, sd 0.71. \(W(1) - W(0.5)\): the other two, also variance 0.5, sd 0.71, independent of \(W(0.5)\). (b) \(\frac1h\) steps of variance \(h^2\): variance \(h\). \(h = 0.25\): 0.25; \(h = 0.01\): 0.01. As \(h \to 0\) the walk stops moving: steps of size \(h\) give no noise in the limit, only steps of size \(\sqrt h\) keep the variance at \(t\). (c) \(\mathrm E\,|\Delta W|/h = 0.80\sqrt h/h = 0.80/\sqrt h\): 8.0 for \(h = 0.01\), 80 for \(h = 10^{-4}\). It grows without bound as \(h \to 0\): Brownian paths have no derivative, which is why an SDE is written with \(dW\) and not \(dW/dt\). (d) After \(n\) frames \(x = \sum_{j=1}^n \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\,Z_j\), a sum of \(n\) independent terms of variance \(2D\Delta t\): variance \(2Dn\Delta t = 2Dt\). In 2D the squared displacement is \(x^2 + y^2\), with mean \(2Dt + 2Dt = 4Dt\).

B2. (a) \(x_1 = 40 - 40 \cdot 0.01 + 30 \cdot 0.1 \cdot (-1.2) = 40 - 0.4 - 3.6 = 36.0\) nm. \(x_2 = 36 - 0.36 + 3 \cdot 0.5 = 37.14\) nm. B3. (a) \(\mathrm E\,x_{n+1} = (1 - \theta h)\,\mathrm E\,x_n\), so \(\mathrm E\,x_n = (1 - \theta h)^n x_0\): decays if \(|1 - \theta h| < 1\), i.e. \(0 < h < 2/\theta\). In the long run both variances equal \(v\): \(v\big(1 - (1 - \theta h)^2\big) = \sigma^2 h\), and \(1 - (1 - \theta h)^2 = 2\theta h - \theta^2h^2 = \theta h(2 - \theta h)\), so \(v = \sigma^2/(\theta(2 - \theta h))\). (c) \(h = 0.1\): \(860/1.9 = 452.6\); \(h = 1\): \(860/1 = 860\); \(h = 1.5\): \(860/0.5 = 1720\). Exact 430. For small \(h\), \(v/430 = 2/(2 - \theta h) \approx 1 + \theta h/2\): a relative error of \(\theta h/2\) (5% at \(h = 0.1\)), first order in \(h\), as the weak order 1 predicts. (d) \(v = e^{-2\theta h}v + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta h})\), so \(v(1 - e^{-2\theta h}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta h})\) and \(v = \sigma^2/(2\theta)\) for every \(h\): no error.

B4. (a) Mean \(1000\,e^{0.1t}\): 2718 after 10 years, 148 413 after 50. Median \(1000\,e^{(0.1 - 0.18)t} = 1000\,e^{-0.08t}\): 449 after 10 years, 18.3 after 50. (d) Factors \(1 + 0.1 + 0.6 = 1.7\) and \(1 + 0.1 - 0.6 = 0.5\). Their mean is 1.1 \(= 1 + r\): the mean grows by 10% per year. But a single population gets both in turn: \(1.7 \cdot 0.5 = 0.85\) over two years, a decline of 8% per year (\(\sqrt{0.85} = 0.92\)). The product (geometric mean) describes one population; the arithmetic mean describes an average over many populations, carried by the lucky ones.

B5. (a) \(\mathrm E\,(1 - kh + \mu\Delta W)^2 = (1 - kh)^2 + 2(1 - kh)\mu\,\mathrm E\,\Delta W + \mu^2\,\mathrm E\,\Delta W^2 = (1 - kh)^2 + \mu^2h\), and \(\Delta W_n\) is independent of \(C_n\), so the expectations multiply. rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

def track(n, u=0.0):                          # n frames, flow u along x (µm/s)
    xy = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(2 * D * dt), (n, 2)), axis=0)
    xy[:, 0] += u * dt * np.arange(1, n + 1)
    return xy

def msd(xy, K):                               # time-averaged MSD at lags 1..K
    return np.array([np.mean(np.sum((xy[k:] - xy[:-k])**2, axis=1)) for k in range(1, K + 1)])

    lag = np.arange(1, K + 1) * dt
    slope = np.sum(lag * msd(xy, K)) / np.sum(lag**2)
    return slope / (2 * xy.shape[1])

// C1: diffusion coefficient of a bead from the mean squared displacement (MSD) of its track.
use rand::{rngs::StdRng, SeedableRng};
use rand_distr::{Distribution, Normal};

const D: f64 = 0.43; // µm^2/s
const DT: f64 = 0.1; // s per frame

// n frames of a 2D track (columns x, y), flow u along x in µm/s
fn track(n: usize, u: f64, rng: &mut StdRng) -> Vec<[f64; 2]> {
    let step = Normal::new(0.0, (2.0 * D * DT).sqrt()).unwrap();
    let (mut x, mut y) = (0.0, 0.0);
    (1..=n)
        .map(|i| {
            x += step.sample(rng);
            y += step.sample(rng);
            [x + u * DT * i as f64, y]
        })
        .collect()
}

// D from a line through 0 fitted to MSD at lags 1..K; dims = 2 (x, y) or 1 (y only)
fn dhat(xy: &[[f64; 2]], k_max: usize, dims: usize) -> f64 {
    let (mut stm, mut stt) = (0.0, 0.0);
    for k in 1..=k_max {
        let n = xy.len() - k;
        let mut msd = 0.0;
        for i in 0..n {
            let dy = xy[i + k][1] - xy[i][1];
            let dx = if dims == 2 { xy[i + k][0] - xy[i][0] } else { 0.0 };
            msd += dx * dx + dy * dy;
        }
        msd /= n as f64;
        let lag = k as f64 * DT;
        stm += lag * msd;
        stt += lag * lag;
    }
    stm / stt / (2.0 * dims as f64) // slope / (2 d)
}

fn mean_sd(v: &[f64]) -> (f64, f64) {
    let n = v.len() as f64;
    let m = v.iter().sum::<f64>() / n;
    (m, (v.iter().map(|x| (x - m).powi(2)).sum::<f64>() / (n - 1.0)).sqrt())
}

fn main() {
    let mut rng = StdRng::seed_from_u64(1);
    let xy = track(100, 0.0, &mut rng);
    println!("K4 {:.4}  K50 {:.4}", dhat(&xy, 4, 2), dhat(&xy, 50, 2));
    for (n, k) in [(100, 4), (100, 50), (1000, 4)] {
        let d: Vec<f64> = (0..1000).map(|_| dhat(&track(n, 0.0, &mut rng), k, 2)).collect();
        let (m, s) = mean_sd(&d);
        println!("n = {:4}  K = {:2}  mean {:.3}  sd {:.3}", n, k, m, s);
    }
    let (mut d2, mut dy) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new());
    for _ in 0..1000 {
        let xy = track(100, 5.0, &mut rng);
        d2.push(dhat(&xy, 4, 2));
        dy.push(dhat(&xy, 4, 1));
    }
    println!("2D with flow {:.4}  y only {:.4}", mean_sd(&d2).0, mean_sd(&dy).0);
}

// C2: strong and weak error of Euler-Maruyama on geometric Brownian motion, same Brownian path.
use rand::{rngs::StdRng, RngExt, SeedableRng};
use rand_distr::StandardNormal;

// least-squares slope of log(err) against log(h)
fn slope(h: &[f64], e: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let (x, y): (Vec<f64>, Vec<f64>) = h.iter().zip(e).map(|(a, b)| (a.ln(), b.ln())).unzip();
    let n = x.len() as f64;
    let (mx, my) = (x.iter().sum::<f64>() / n, y.iter().sum::<f64>() / n);
    let sxy: f64 = x.iter().zip(&y).map(|(a, b)| (a - mx) * (b - my)).sum();
    sxy / x.iter().map(|a| (a - mx).powi(2)).sum::<f64>()
}

fn main() {
    let (r, s, n0, t) = (0.8f64, 0.5f64, 1.0f64, 1.0f64);
    let (m, nf) = (10000usize, 1024usize);
    let hf = t / nf as f64;
    let rs: Vec<usize> = (0..9).map(|k| 1usize << k).collect(); // coarse step h = R hf
    let mut rng = StdRng::seed_from_u64(1);
    let (mut strong, mut diff) = (vec![0.0; rs.len()], vec![0.0; rs.len()]);
    let mut dwf = vec![0.0f64; nf];
    for _ in 0..m {
        for w in dwf.iter_mut() {
            *w = hf.sqrt() * rng.sample::<f64, _>(StandardNormal); // fine increments
        }
        let nt = n0 * ((r - s * s / 2.0) * t + s * dwf.iter().sum::<f64>()).exp(); // exact N(T)
        for (j, &rr) in rs.iter().enumerate() {
            let h = rr as f64 * hf;
            let mut n = n0;
            for chunk in dwf.chunks(rr) {
                let dw: f64 = chunk.iter().sum(); // sum R fine increments
                n += r * n * h + s * n * dw;
            }
            strong[j] += (n - nt).abs() / m as f64;
            diff[j] += (n - nt) / m as f64;
        }
    }
    let h: Vec<f64> = rs.iter().map(|&rr| rr as f64 * hf).collect();
    let weak: Vec<f64> = diff.iter().map(|d| d.abs()).collect();
    for j in 0..rs.len() {
        println!("h = {:.7}  strong {:.5}  weak {:.6}", h[j], strong[j], weak[j]);
    }
    println!("slopes: strong {:.4}  weak {:.4}", slope(&h, &strong), slope(&h, &weak));

// C6: Fokker-Planck PDE (flux form, explicit Euler) against a histogram's moments of EM paths.
use rand::{rngs::StdRng, RngExt, SeedableRng};
use rand_distr::{Distribution, Normal, StandardNormal};

const THETA: f64 = 1.0; // per ms
const D: f64 = 430.0; // nm^2/ms

// mass, mean and variance of the PDE solution at t_end
fn fp(t_end: f64, dx: f64, dt: f64, upwind: bool) -> [f64; 3] {
    let n = ((260.0 / dx).round() as usize) + 1;
    let x: Vec<f64> = (0..n).map(|i| -100.0 + i as f64 * dx).collect();
    let u: Vec<f64> = (0..n - 1).map(|i| -THETA * (x[i + 1] - dx / 2.0)).collect(); // at the faces
    let mut p: Vec<f64> = x.iter()
        .map(|&xi| (-(xi - 60.0).powi(2) / 50.0).exp() / (2.0 * std::f64::consts::PI * 25.0).sqrt())
        .collect();
    for _ in 0..(t_end / dt).round() as usize {
        let f: Vec<f64> = (0..n - 1).map(|i| {
            let pf = if upwind { if u[i] > 0.0 { p[i] } else { p[i + 1] } } else { (p[i] + p[i + 1]) / 2.0 };
            u[i] * pf - D * (p[i + 1] - p[i]) / dx // drift + Fick
        }).collect();
        for i in 0..n {
            let fr = if i < n - 1 { f[i] } else { 0.0 }; // no flux through the ends
            let fl = if i > 0 { f[i - 1] } else { 0.0 };
            p[i] -= dt / dx * (fr - fl);
        }
    }
    let mass: f64 = p.iter().sum::<f64>() * dx;
    let mean: f64 = x.iter().zip(&p).map(|(a, b)| a * b).sum::<f64>() * dx;
    let var: f64 = x.iter().zip(&p).map(|(a, b)| (a - mean).powi(2) * b).sum::<f64>() * dx;
    [mass, mean, var]
}

fn main() {
    let sigma = (2.0 * D).sqrt();
    let mut rng = StdRng::seed_from_u64(1);
    let start = Normal::new(60.0, 5.0).unwrap();
    let mut x: Vec<f64> = (0..10000).map(|_| start.sample(&mut rng)).collect();
    let h = 0.01;
    for i in 1..=300 {
        for xi in x.iter_mut() {
            let z: f64 = rng.sample(StandardNormal);
            *xi += -THETA * *xi * h + sigma * h.sqrt() * z;
        }
        if i == 100 || i == 300 {
            let t = i as f64 * h;
            let n = x.len() as f64;
            let m = x.iter().sum::<f64>() / n;
            let v = x.iter().map(|a| (a - m).powi(2)).sum::<f64>() / (n - 1.0);
            let ex = [1.0, 60.0 * (-THETA * t).exp(),
                      D / THETA * (1.0 - (-2.0 * THETA * t).exp()) + 25.0 * (-2.0 * THETA * t).exp()];
            println!("t = {}: mass, mean, var", t);
            println!("  pde     {:?}", fp(t, 2.0, 0.002, false).map(|v| (v * 1e4).round() / 1e4));
            println!("  upwind  {:?}", fp(t, 2.0, 0.002, true).map(|v| (v * 1e4).round() / 1e4));
            println!("  em      {:?}", [1.0, (m * 1e4).round() / 1e4, (v * 1e4).round() / 1e4]);
            println!("  exact   {:?}", ex.map(|v| (v * 1e4).round() / 1e4));
        }
    }
}

// C7: mean-square stability: Euler-Maruyama vs drift-implicit EM for noisy drug clearance.
use rand::{rngs::StdRng, RngExt, SeedableRng};
use rand_distr::StandardNormal;

fn main() {
    let (k, mu, c0, p) = (0.8f64, 1.0f64, 10.0f64, 10000usize); // per hour; 10 000 patients, 48 h
    let mut rng = StdRng::seed_from_u64(1);
    for h in [0.5f64, 1.0, 2.0] {
        let (mut ce, mut ci) = (vec![c0; p], vec![c0; p]);
        for _ in 0..(48.0 / h).round() as usize {
            for j in 0..p {
                let dw = h.sqrt() * rng.sample::<f64, _>(StandardNormal);
                ce[j] += -k * ce[j] * h + mu * ce[j] * dw; // Euler-Maruyama
                ci[j] = (ci[j] + mu * ci[j] * dw) / (1.0 + k * h); // drift-implicit EM
            }
        }
        let max = |v: &Vec<f64>| v.iter().fold(0.0f64, |a, &b| a.max(b.abs()));
        let m2 = ce.iter().map(|c| c * c).sum::<f64>() / p as f64;
        println!("h = {:<3} EM: factor {:.3} max {:.1e}  mean C^2 {:.1e} | implicit: factor {:.3} max {:.1e}",
                 h, (1.0 - k * h).powi(2) + mu * mu * h, max(&ce), m2,
                 (1.0 + mu * mu * h) / (1.0 + k * h).powi(2), max(&ci));
    }
    println!("exact mean C^2 {:.5e}", c0 * c0 * ((-2.0 * k + mu * mu) * 48.0).exp());
}

At \(h = 2\) h, a step at which Euler for the ODE is perfectly stable (factor \(-0.6\)), EM sends some patients from 10 mg/L to between \(10^5\) and \(10^7\) mg/L, and the sample mean of \(C^2\) is \(10^8\)–\(10^{11}\) instead of \(3 \times 10^{-11}\). At \(h = 1\) (factor 1.04) the growth is slow and hidden: the largest value (0.1) still looks harmless, but the mean square of the method decays no longer. The drift-implicit method decays at every \(h\) (largest value 0.002–0.015 at \(h = 2\)). At \(h = 0.5\) both are stable; the sample means of \(C^2\) there are far below the factor prediction \(100 \cdot 0.86^{96} \approx 5 \times 10^{-5}\), because a mean square that decays on average is still carried by rare paths that 10 000 samples do not contain. Mean-square stability is a property you check with the factor (B5), not with a sample.
