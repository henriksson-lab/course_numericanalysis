Quiz: flow plus diffusion

Short questions to test your understanding of advection–diffusion and its discretisation. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. A channel is 200 µm wide and carries a mean flow of 500 µm/s. A drug has \(D = 500\) µm²/s. Compute the Péclet number across the channel and the distance after which two side-by-side streams are mixed to about 1 % (use \(0.42\,\bar u w^2/D\)).

Hint

\(\text{Pe} = \bar u w/D\). Keep everything in µm and s.

Answer

\(\text{Pe} = 500\cdot 200/500 = 200\). Mixing length \(0.42\cdot 500\cdot 200^2/500 = 0.42\cdot 40\,000 = 16\,800\) µm, about 17 mm: 84 channel widths. With Pe ≫ 1, streams flow side by side a long way.

Q2. You solve the steady problem \(u\,c' = D\,c''\) with central differences, \(u = 100\) µm/s, \(D = 1000\) µm²/s, \(\Delta x = 30\) µm. Do you expect wiggles? What is the largest \(\Delta x\) that avoids them?

Hint

Compute the cell Péclet number \(P = u\Delta x/D\) and look at the sign of the coefficient \(1 - P/2\).

Answer

\(P = 100\cdot 30/1000 = 3 > 2\): the coefficient of \(c_{i+1}\) is \(1 - 1.5 < 0\), the recurrence has a negative root \(\rho = (1 + 1.5)/(1 - 1.5) = -5\), and the solution alternates in sign near the boundary layer. Wiggles disappear for \(P \le 2\), i.e. \(\Delta x \le 2D/u = 20\) µm; for second-order accuracy you want \(P\) well below 2.

Q3. An upwind simulation of a protein (\(D = 100\) µm²/s) in a channel with \(u = 1000\) µm/s on a 2 µm grid looks smooth and plausible. Why should you not trust it yet?

Hint

Upwind = central + something. How large is that something here?

Answer

Upwind adds a numerical diffusion \(u\Delta x/2 = 1000\cdot 2/2 = 1000\) µm²/s, ten times the protein’s real \(D\). The simulation is (nearly) the solution for \(D = 1100\) µm²/s: it spreads the protein like a small molecule. Smoothness is no evidence of accuracy. Check by refining the grid (the answer must stop changing) or by comparing \(D_\text{num}\) with \(D\) before running.

Q4. A simulated dye concentration contains values of \(-0.2\) next to a steep front. Name two different numerical causes, and a quick check that tells them apart.

Hint

One cause is in the steady discrete equations, one in the time stepping.

Answer
  1. Central differences with cell Péclet number \(u\Delta x/D > 2\): the discrete equations have an oscillating solution. The wiggles have a fixed size and do not grow in time (they appear in a steady solve too). (2) An unstable explicit time step, \(C + 2r > 1\) (or \(r > 1/2\), or \(C > 1\)): a zigzag that grows step by step until it overflows. Check: compute \(P\), \(C\) and \(r\), and watch \(\max|c|\) over time: bounded means (1), growing means (2). In both cases the fix is a finer grid or (for 2) a smaller or implicit step.

Q5. Explicit upwind with \(u = 500\) µm/s, \(D = 1000\) µm²/s, \(\Delta x = 5\) µm. What is the largest stable time step? Is it set by advection or by diffusion?

Hint

\(C = u\Delta t/\Delta x\), \(r = D\Delta t/\Delta x^2\), and the condition is \(C + 2r \le 1\).

Answer

\(C = 100\,\Delta t\) and \(r = 40\,\Delta t\), so \(C + 2r = 180\,\Delta t \le 1\): \(\Delta t \le 5.6\) ms. Both matter: advection alone would allow 10 ms (\(C \le 1\)), diffusion alone 12.5 ms (\(r \le 1/2\)). On fine grids diffusion wins, since \(r\) grows like \(1/\Delta x^2\) and \(C\) only like \(1/\Delta x\).

Q6. In the T-sensor we solved a steady 2D problem by “marching” in \(x\) as if \(x\) were time. What assumption makes this possible, and when would it fail?

Hint

Which term did we drop, and what does that do to the direction in which information travels?

Answer

We dropped diffusion along the flow, \(D\,\partial^2 c/\partial x^2\), which is about \(1/\text{Pe}^2\) times smaller than advection when \(\text{Pe} \gg 1\). Then the concentration at \(x\) depends only on what happened upstream, like a solution at time \(t\) depends only on earlier times. It fails when Pe is not large (diffusion upstream matters, as in the back-diffusion problem), when the flow reverses or recirculates (eddies, dead-end side chambers), or where \(u = 0\) along a whole region. Then one solves the full steady problem as one sparse linear system (Steady state in two dimensions).

Q7. Why is explicit marching impractical for the T-sensor with a Poiseuille profile, even though it works well with plug flow?

Hint

The “time step” in row \(j\) is \(\Delta x/u_j\).

Answer

Explicit marching needs \(D\,\Delta x/(u_j\Delta y^2) \le 1/2\) in every row, so \(\Delta x \le u_j\Delta y^2/(2D)\). Near the walls \(u_j \to 0\), so the step must shrink towards 0: with 2 µm cells it is 0.12 µm in the first row against 3 µm in the middle, 168 000 steps for 20 mm. It is stiffness again: the slowest fluid sets the step for everyone. Backward Euler (one tridiagonal solve per step) has no such limit.

Q8. An H-filter extracts 45 % of a small molecule in a 1.6 mm channel at \(\bar u = 1000\) µm/s. You double the flow rate to process more sample. How long must the channel be for the same extraction?

Hint

The marching equation depends on \(D\), \(\bar u\) and \(x\) only through one combination.

Answer

Only \(Dx/\bar u\) matters, so doubling \(\bar u\) needs twice the length: 3.2 mm. The protein carried along stays the same fraction (17 %), since it obeys the same scaling. The channel length per unit throughput is fixed by \(D\).

Q9. In the simplified Christmas-tree model, new channel \(k\) in stage \(n \to n + 1\) takes \(k/n\) of upstream stream \(k - 1\) and \((n - k)/n\) of stream \(k\). Starting from inlets \((0, 1)\), what are the concentrations after two stages? What does the model assume about each serpentine?

Hint

First stage: \(n = 2\), three channels.

Answer

\((0, 1) \to (0, 0.5, 1) \to (0, \tfrac13, \tfrac23, 1)\): a linear staircase, and it stays linear at every stage. The model assumes each serpentine mixes its two inputs completely, i.e. it is longer than about \(0.4\,\bar u w^2/D\) for the molecule used. For a slowly diffusing protein at high flow that may fail, and the outlet profile then differs from the design.

Q10. You fit a diffusion coefficient to a T-sensor profile with the marching model. On a coarse grid (\(\Delta x = 1\) mm, one step) the fit gives \(D = 505\) µm²/s; on fine grids it settles at 396. Why is the coarse estimate too high, and how do you know when the grid is fine enough?

Hint

What does a coarse backward-Euler step do to the profile, compared with the exact solution?

Answer

Backward Euler damps each Fourier mode (rate \(\lambda\)) by \(1/(1 + \lambda\Delta x)\) per step instead of the exact \(e^{-\lambda\Delta x}\), and \(1/(1 + z) > e^{-z}\). So one coarse step spreads the interface too little; to reproduce the measured spreading the optimiser must raise \(D\). The fitted parameter absorbs the discretisation error, here 27 % upwards. Refit on a sequence of finer grids until the fitted \(D\) stops changing to the precision you need (here \(\Delta x \le 25\) µm): the same grid-refinement check as for any simulation, applied to the estimate.

Q11. The flow rate in your T-sensor experiment is only known to ±10 %. How precisely can you estimate \(D\) from downstream profiles, however many profiles you measure?

Hint

Which parameter combination do the profiles determine?

Answer

To about ±10 %. The profiles depend only on \(D/\bar u\) (times \(x\)), so \(D\) and \(\bar u\) are not separately identifiable: a 10 % error in \(\bar u\) is a 10 % error in \(D\). More profiles or less noise shrink the uncertainty of \(D/\bar u\), not that of \(\bar u\). Measure the flow rate independently (calibrated pump, weighing the outflow, bead tracking).
